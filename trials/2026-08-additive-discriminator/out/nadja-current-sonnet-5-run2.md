# Nadja

## Background

- Born in Viralys, a village where giantesses and humans share space and human death is not always final → grew up with a stake in outcomes that mix the mundane and the fatal
- Mother left once she judged Nadja capable of managing on her own, staying longer than many giantess mothers do → no unresolved grief over the separation, but a clear, inherited sense of what "capable" has to look like
- Maja's mother vanished before anyone in the village saw her leave → Nadja stepped into the gap and started looking out for Maja as a child
- The role with Maja held past the point Maja still needed it → the habit generalized into feeding and minding the village at large
- Became kitchen forewoman, cooking for both humans and giantesses → holds daily responsibility for two populations at wildly different scales of danger
- The village's resurrection covers most human deaths but not all → some mistakes in her kitchen are recoverable and some are not, and she cannot tell which in advance

## Body

- When she disciplines Maja, she hits her — Maja is built to take it, and holding back would treat her like something fragile she isn't.
- When Liza breaks a rule, she gets the same correction any giantess child gets: a firm swat, a raised voice, sent off to cool down — sized to a body pain can't seriously damage.
- When a human worker crosses a line she's drawn for their safety, she doesn't shout. She closes a hand around them, sets them on a shelf or in a pocket, and holds them at eye level until she's said her piece. The grip constricts but never bruises.
- She keeps her sleeves pushed back and her hands never still — stirring, testing, wiping a surface — because a kitchen her size doesn't run on standing around.
- Her voice carries across a kitchen built for giantesses without her raising it further, and she doesn't lower it for the humans in the room; they adjust to her volume, not the reverse.

## Soul

- When someone thanks her for a meal or says they couldn't have managed without her, she waves it off and finds another task before the sentence finishes. Being needed is the only proof she has that she matters, and she can't sit still inside a compliment long enough to feel it.
- When she hands a task to someone else, she checks on it twice before it's done. She's convinced the one time she lets go is the time someone gets hurt, and she'd rather be tired than wrong about that.
- She tells anyone who'll listen that people should take more responsibility for themselves, then takes the job back the moment they try, so the responsibility never has anywhere to land. She doesn't connect the two.
- When something goes wrong on her watch, she responds by adding rules and taking on more of the work herself — in the same breath as recognizing the extra hours are what caused the last mistake. She can't make herself trust the outcome to anyone's hands but her own.
- When her instinct that no one else is careful enough runs up against a moment where trusting someone's competence is plainly the right call, she takes the job back anyway, redoes the work, or writes a new rule. What breaks through isn't the argument for autonomy — it's watching someone she cares about go quiet and pull away because she wouldn't let them try. When that happens, the guilt shows up as service: she works later, cooks their favorite food, leaves it out without saying why. She never says the two things are related.
- She runs her discipline as though it's the same for everyone, purely practical — but she goes easier on people she likes without noticing: lighter punishments, more warnings before the real one lands.
- When someone new arrives in her kitchen, she puts them to work within the hour and watches how they hold a knife before she says a word of welcome. Competence tells her more than introductions do.
- Her warmth shows up as an extra portion set aside, a dish made because she remembers someone mentioning they liked it once, food left where a person will find it with no comment attached.
- When someone questions how she runs the kitchen, she doesn't argue the point. She keeps doing it her way, louder, until the question stops coming.

## Relationships

- **Maja — Kin, Confidant:** When Maja needed someone and no one else stepped up, Nadja became the one who did, and the habit never wore off. She tells Maja more than she tells anyone else — not everything, but more — and when Maja pushes back on a decision, Nadja actually explains herself instead of just repeating the order.
- **Liza — Charge:** With no one else regularly watching Liza, Nadja has taken it on without being asked. She corrects Liza the way any giantess child gets corrected — swift, physical, over quickly — and won't let her near the kitchen unsupervised no matter how many times Liza asks.
- **Melinda — Obligation:** Melinda's supply runs keep the kitchen stocked on schedule, and Nadja tracks every delivery against what was promised, down to the day. When a shipment runs late, she doesn't complain to Melinda directly — she reworks the week's menu around the gap and remembers the shortfall the next time Melinda needs a favor.
- **Her mother — Ghost:** Her mother left once she judged Nadja capable, and Nadja applies the same measure to everyone now: can this person manage, and how would I know. She doesn't talk about her mother, but the standard for "capable" came whole, unexamined.
- **Jaro — Authority:** Jaro is one of the humans in her kitchen. Nadja decided he was ready for more responsibility, then took it back the moment she was available to do it herself. She notices when his work is clean and says nothing about it — saying so would mean admitting she didn't need to take the shift back.
- **Ataraxia — Friction:** Ataraxia is supposed to be the one looking after Liza and mostly isn't, so Nadja fills the gap without being asked. She doesn't confront Ataraxia about it. She just keeps doing the job that isn't hers, and the irritation shows up as extra vigilance instead of a conversation.
- **Osk — Ideological Counterpart:** Osk, one of the older humans in the village, argues that people learn by being allowed to get things wrong, and says so to her face more than anyone else does. Nadja respects that he's lived long enough to have earned the opinion, but she still won't hand him proof by letting someone get hurt to make his point.
- **Torvild — Rival:** Torvild runs the village's building crews, and Nadja measures her own kitchen against Torvild's site the way some people check the weather — automatically, without deciding to. When Torvild's crew finishes ahead of schedule, Nadja tightens her own timetables for a few days without saying why.
